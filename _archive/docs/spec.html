Hackathon spec · Monad Blitz Oregon · Sunday 15 November 2026 · working name

Lantern

A shared map of lunar ice that rovers write themselves. Each rover drives through a dark crater by recognising craters in its lamp light, reports ice with how sure it is of the location, and checks the reports of other agencies' rovers. The map lives on Monad, so no agency owns it and none can change another's entries.

The goal

When a rover reports ice on the Moon, anyone should be able to see the report, see how precise its location is, and see whether another agency has gone and checked it.

Lantern simulates that: two rovers from two agencies, navigating in the dark, writing finds and checks to one public record. It is built to do three things at once.

  • Win Monad Blitz with a project where the chain does real work: it holds the record and enforces who may check what.
  • Give PROSPECT the dark-driving navigation sim its RASC-AL proposal needs (area 3, due 24 February 2027).
  • Show that a find is only as useful as its location. A find whose location is 20 m uncertain cannot be confirmed, so better navigation makes the shared map trustworthy.

The problem

  • Rovers in shadowed craters are driven from Earth. VIPER's wheels turn at 10 cm/s, but it averages about 0.8 cm/s because people plan each move. PROSPECT's Scout has to average about 7.4 cm/s, roughly nine times faster.
  • Wheel and visual odometry drift about 2% of the distance driven, so after 500 m the position error passes 10 m. Today a person on Earth re-anchors the rover by matching its images to orbital maps.
  • ShadowNav (JPL, 2023) showed in simulation that a rover can do this itself in the dark. A lamp below the camera puts a shadow on the near rim of each crater, and the rover matches those rims to craters on the orbital map.
  • Several nations plan to prospect the same few shadowed craters. The Artemis Accords ask signatories to share scientific data openly (section 8). They do not require reports of prospecting finds, since their resource section covers extraction (section 10), so the pitch should not claim they do.
  • No shared record says where ice has been found, how precisely, and whether anyone else has confirmed it.

How it works

Each rover runs the same loop every few metres: see, locate, plan, drive, measure. When it finds ice or checks someone else's find, it posts that to the contract. Before it plans, it reads other agencies' finds from the chain so it can go and check them.

The demo, about three minutes

Two space agencies, A and B, each run one rover in the same dark crater. The demo runs twice. The first run uses crater-based navigation. The second run turns it off for rover A, and that run shows why navigation matters to the record.

  1. Rover A drives in the dark. The camera view shows crater rims picked out by the lamp, and on the map A's estimate stays on its true track while plain odometry drifts away.
  2. A finds ice and posts it: where it is, how sure A is of that spot, and the sensor reading. The find shows on the dashboard and on the Monad block explorer as reported.
  3. B, from the other agency, reads the find from the chain, drives to it with its own navigation and measures. The ice is there, so B posts a check that agrees, and the status changes to confirmed.
  4. Run 2, with A's crater fixes off. After a kilometre of odometry alone A is about 20 m unsure of its position, so the ice could be anywhere in a circle 40 m wide. B measures at the reported spot, finds nothing, and posts a check that disagrees. The find stays on the record, marked disputed.

Why a blockchain

  • No single owner. Rival agencies can all write to it, and none can edit or delete another's entry. A record on one agency's server would depend on trusting that agency.
  • The rules run on the chain. Only a different agency can check a find, and only from close to where it was reported. The contract sets the status, and no agency can override it.
  • Disagreements stay public. A disputed find is kept and marked, which is how MEWChain treats failed replications.

The chain cannot prove a rover was where it says. That is why each find carries its uncertainty, and why trust comes from independent checks.

The parts, in build order

The program is six parts, built in this order. The step numbers match the tags on the flowchart. One person can build all six in about six hours, and the demo runs end to end after step 5. With more people, each takes a step, and the "takes" and "gives" lines are the hand-offs between them.

Step 1 · 45 minChain

Does
Keeps the shared record and enforces the checking rules.
Takes
Finds and checks, posted from Python.
Gives
Every find with its status: reported, confirmed or disputed. The functions are in the table below.
How
A Solidity contract on Monad testnet, built and tested with Foundry. A Python client with web3.py. One throwaway testnet key per agency, never committed.
Done
A find and a check post from Python and read back, and the contract refuses a check that breaks the rules.

Step 2 · 30 minTerrain

Does
Makes the crater floor and the orbital crater map.
Gives
A height grid with 0.25 m cells, and a crater list (centre and diameter). The crater list is what the rover's orbital map contains.
How
Rolling noise terrain plus craters drawn from a size distribution. A real LOLA tile can replace the noise later (see upgrades). The output stays the same, so nothing else changes.
Done
A top-down plot shows the terrain with its mapped craters.

Step 3 · 90 minNavigation

Does
Works out where the rover is.
Takes
Odometry each step, with 2% drift (from a scripted drive until step 4). Rim points around the rover. The crater list.
Gives
A position estimate and its uncertainty, every step.
How
ShadowNav's particle filter: 100 guesses of the position, each scored by how close the seen rims fall to mapped craters (the Q-Score), resampled when fewer than 50 guesses carry the weight. Heading is assumed known, as from a star tracker. Until step 6, the rim points are stand-ins: points on the near rims of mapped craters within 15 m of the true position, with noise added and some missed, in the same format vision will give.
Done
The estimate stays on the true track while odometry alone drifts. The log shows the filter's reported uncertainty next to its true error, because on trajectory 1 ShadowNav's filter reported 1.84 m against a true error of 3.84 m (Table 2).

Step 4 · 60 minRover loop

Does
Runs the two rovers, one per agency, and decides what each does next.
Takes
Each rover's estimate, the height grid, and the finds read from the chain.
Gives
Each rover's true pose and odometry every step, its readings, and the finds and checks to post.
How
Route search on a slope map with a 15° limit, toward new ground or another agency's find. A simulated ice sensor over a hidden ice map. A strong reading becomes a find. A reading at another agency's find becomes a check that agrees or disagrees.
Done
Both rovers finish a run on stand-in rims, one posting a find and the other checking it.

Step 5 · 45 minDashboard

Does
Shows the judges what is happening.
Takes
The sim's state each step, the contract's events, and the camera image once step 6 makes it.
Gives
One window: the map with the true, odometry and estimated tracks and every find, a feed of chain transactions, and the camera view with rims marked. Plus a backup video of a good run.
Done
A full run plays live, end to end.

Step 6 · 90 minVision

Does
Shows what the rover's camera sees in the dark and finds the crater rims in it. Replaces the stand-in rims.
Takes
The height grid and each rover's true pose (position and heading).
Gives
The camera image, and rim points in metres relative to the rover.
How
Raycast the height grid from the camera. The lamp sits below the camera, and shadows come from a second cast from the lamp's position, compared by depth. Canny finds the shadow lines, and the renderer's depth, with noise added, places each rim point.
Done
Rim points from any pose line up with the mapped craters, the filter runs on them, and the dashboard shows the camera view.
Risk
The step most likely to run over, so it comes last and the demo runs without it. ShadowNav's authors found bugs in this same projection pipeline by testing on exact rim points, which is what the stand-ins are (section 6C). A second person should start it at build start.
Contract functions
postFindPosition, uncertainty, ice reading and a hash of the raw data. Returns a find ID. The status starts as reported.
checkFindA find ID, the checker's position and uncertainty, its reading, and agree or disagree. Refused if the checker is the same agency as the finder, or if it measured farther from the find than three times the two uncertainties added together.
getFindEverything about a find, the agencies that agree and disagree, and its status: disputed if any agency disagrees, confirmed if at least one agrees and none disagree.

Positions are in south polar stereographic metres, the same frame as the LOLA maps, stored as whole centimetres. Each agency is one signing address, fixed when the contract is deployed. Any other address is refused, so no one can confirm their own find from a second wallet.

Where the method comes from

From ShadowNav: the lamp below the camera (section 3A, Figure 3), Canny rim detection beyond 10 m with 80% of the near arc found at 10 m and some arcs out to 20 m (sections 1, 5 and 7), and the particle filter with the Q-Score and systematic resampling (Algorithms 1 to 3, settings in section 6B). Simplified for one day: plain lamp shading instead of the Hapke lunar reflectance model, depth from the renderer instead of stereo matching, no near-range depth-jump detector, and heading assumed known.
