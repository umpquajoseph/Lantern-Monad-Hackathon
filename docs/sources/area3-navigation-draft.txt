4. Navigation and Autonomy in Permanent Shadow

Orbital neutron data locate polar hydrogen at a resolution of 10 to 20 km, whereas planning extraction
requires resource maps finer than 100 m, confirmed by measurements on the ground [1]. The Scouts must
therefore cover several kilometres of the floor of a permanently shadowed region (PSR) on each battery
charge and tie every measurement to a known position. PROSPECT uses an existing crater-based
localization method [2] and adds three elements suited to a campaign run from a single dock: (1) a crater
map refined over repeated sorties, (2) a resource map that records the position confidence of every
measurement, and (3) localizability as a criterion for selecting the target crater.

4.1 Speed and the Stop Budget

VIPER's wheels were designed to turn at 10 cm/s, but because each drive segment is planned by operators
on Earth, its average speed over a mission day is about 0.8 cm/s [ref]8. The Scouts instead operate in
sorties, round trips from the charging dock on the crater rim into the PSR and back. A baseline sortie,
consisting of a 1.5 km descent, a 4 km survey with drilling at two sites, and the return, covers about 7 km
in 22 h. This is an average of 8.8 cm/s1, roughly eleven times VIPER's rate, and requires the Scouts to
plan and execute their own drives.

We call the time a Scout may spend stationary during a sortie its stop budget. It follows from the top
speed: at 10, 15 and 20 cm/s the Scout spends 19.4, 13.0 and 9.7 h moving, leaving 2.6, 9.0 and 12.3 h for
drilling, position fixes, hazard stops and revisits. We baseline a top speed of 15 cm/s2, which gives a stop
budget of 9.0 h.

4.2 Localization and Its Limit

Relative localization from odometry drifts by about 2% of the distance travelled [2], or about 140 m over
a sortie. The Scouts bound this drift with ShadowNav, developed at JPL [2]. A lamp mounted below the
stereo cameras casts a shadow beyond the near rim of each crater ahead, and a particle filter matches the
detected rims to craters in an orbital elevation map, giving an absolute position fix. Over three simulated
traverses the mean final error was 1.68 to 3.84 m [2]. Each sortie starts from the dock, whose position is
registered to the orbital map15; any error in that registration is shared by every sortie and by both Scouts.

We model the error between fixes as e(d) = efix + 0.02d, where d is the distance driven since the last fix,
and set limits on the 95th-percentile radial error, applied along the whole sortie as the conservative case.
We take the 95th percentile of the error after a fix as the mean plus 1.645 standard deviations across
ShadowNav's 25 runs per traverse [2]. Table 1 gives the resulting maximum spacing between fixes.
ShadowNav's largest errors arose on a traverse where craters missing from the orbital map produced false
matches, in which detected rims were paired with the wrong mapped crater [2]. On that traverse the filter's
reported uncertainty averaged 1.84 m against an average error of 3.84 m, so the Scouts schedule fixes by
distance driven rather than by the filter's own estimate. With a 10 m limit, the traverse with false matches
requires a fix every 80 m, about 88 per sortie, and the best traverse a fix every 358 m, about 20 per

The higher rate conflicts with the stop budget. It has not been shown whether a fix requires the Scout to
stop11; if each fix takes five minutes stationary, 88 fixes consume 7.3 of the 9.0 h available, leaving 1.7 h
for drilling at two sites, hazard stops and revisits, whereas 20 fixes take 1.7 h. The completeness of the
crater map, which sets the false-match rate, may therefore determine whether a sortie can be completed at
all. The three elements below address it.

4.3 A Crater Map Refined over Repeated Sorties

The orbital elevation models available for PSRs resolve 0.5 to 5 m per pixel [2], so many craters the
Scouts detect are absent from the map. Adding newly observed landmarks to a map and reusing them on
later traverses is the basis of simultaneous localization and mapping (SLAM), and of the teach-and-repeat
navigation demonstrated for planetary rovers [5]13. PROSPECT's operations suit this approach: each
Scout makes about 16 sorties a month from one dock, along a shared descent corridor and adjacent survey
lines, so most ground is seen many times. Each Scout logs the estimated position and size of every rim it
cannot match. At the dock, these detections are compared across sorties and between Scouts, and a crater
enters the shared map only after independent sightings agree12. A crater added in this way inherits the
position error of the Scouts that observed it, and its size must be estimated from a partial rim, which
ShadowNav has not demonstrated; the Monte Carlo model includes both effects. Refinement follows the
workshop recommendation that resource maps be developed and then refined throughout the campaign
[1], and it reduces the false-match rate, and with it the number of fixes per sortie, as the campaign
proceeds (Figure 1b).

4.4 A Resource Map That Records Position Confidence

Terrestrial mining classifies resources by the confidence of the evidence behind them, and a lunar
adaptation of this practice has been proposed [6]13. For a lunar deposit, confidence also depends on how
well its position is known. Each Scout therefore stores every neutron spectrometer and radar reading with
its estimated position and 95th-percentile position bound. When a reading exceeds the threshold for a
candidate deposit, the other Scout surveys the surrounding area on its next sortie, out to a radius set by
both Scouts' position bounds, and the deposit is confirmed if a matching reading is recorded there. One
robot finding a resource and another returning to it was also used in [3]. Because both Scouts navigate by
the same map from the same dock, their errors are not independent: the check is meaningful only when
the combined bound is well below the deposit radius and the spectrometer footprint14, and errors common
to both Scouts, such as the dock registration, must be bounded separately. The resource map distinguishes
single readings, confirmed deposits and drilled deposits, and drilling is reserved for confirmed deposits, so
that each drill sample provides ground truth for a deposit already located twice [1].

4.5 Localizability in Site Selection

Section 4.2 suggests that a crater floor with few mapped craters may not be surveyable within the stop
budget. Localizability maps derived from orbital imagery have been used in rover path planning [7]13; we
apply the same measure to the choice of target PSR. Crater density at each candidate is counted from
orbital imagery16, and the Monte Carlo model gives the minimum density at which each fix policy meets
the drill-site limit (Figure 1a).

4.6 Simulation, Sensors and Operations

We are developing a Monte Carlo model of complete sorties in which the errors published in [2] are
sampled as inputs. It compares fix policies, with and without map refinement, across ranges of crater
density, false-match rate, odometry drift from 1 to 4%, sensor failure and map error. It reports the
probability of returning with the 25% battery reserve, the 95th-percentile error at drill sites, and the energy
spent on the lamp and computer. The team's simulator, Lantern, which drives two rovers over the Lunar
Orbiter Laser Altimeter (LOLA) elevation map of Shoemaker crater [4], will demonstrate map refinement
and deposit checks and serve as the virtual prototype. It is not a source of performance figures.

Each Scout carries a stereo camera pair with a lamp below it, a forward lidar for hazards and slopes,
wheel odometry, and a star tracker and inertial measurement unit5 for heading, which crater matching
does not provide [2]. Their mass, power, volume and data rates are given in the system budget17. Although
the battery holds 7.9 kWh, the dock's 3 h recharge at 1.5 kW supplies 4.5 kWh, which limits the average
load to about 205 W3. Contact with Earth may be lost for up to 11 days, about eight sortie cycles, during
which the Scouts and dock store about 30 GB of data until it can be sent18 and each Scout follows rules
fixed before launch, such as turning back when its energy falls to the amount needed to reach the dock
with the reserve. The method requires darkness, a lamp, a star tracker and an orbital map with enough
craters, and so applies to night-time operation elsewhere on the Moon wherever such maps exist.

4.7 Technology Readiness and Risk
ShadowNav's crater detection has been tested on real night-time stereo images and the complete method
in simulation [2], which we assess as TRL 3. A later field test [8] may support TRL 4.9 Map refinement
and deposit checks are operating procedures built on it and are at TRL 2, and adding craters from partial
rims is less mature than ShadowNav's matching. The principal risks are a crater floor with too few
mapped craters, errors entering the refined map, a dock registration error shared by both Scouts, and lamp
and computer loads beyond the energy budget. They are addressed respectively by crater density as a site-
selection criterion, by admitting craters to the map only after independent sightings agree, by the choice of
registration method, and by the Monte Carlo energy accounting.
